Coupled Cluster Gradients on One Page

What to be able to write down and what to be able to answer. Everything here is derived in the Coupled Cluster Gradients notes; the equation numbers in brackets point there.

Notation. \(\ket{0}\) is the Hartree–Fock reference, \(\ket{\mu}\), \(\ket{\nu}\) are the excited determinants inside the truncation, \(\tau_\mu\ket{0}=\ket{\mu}\), \(\Hb=e^{-T}He^{T}\), \(x\) is a nuclear coordinate, \(A^{x}\) a full derivative and \(A^{(x)}\) a skeleton derivative with unperturbed orbitals. Occupied \(i,j\), virtual \(a,b\), general \(p,q\).

The four equations

$$\mathcal L(t,\lambda,x)=\bra{0}\Hb\ket{0}+\sum_{\mu}\lambda_\mu\bra{\mu}\Hb\ket{0}=\bra{0}(1+\Lambda)\,\Hb\,\ket{0},\qquad \Lambda=\sum_\mu\lambda_\mu\tau_\mu^\dagger .\tag{1}$$

THE LAGRANGIAN [7]. The energy plus the amplitude equations times multipliers. It equals \(E\) whenever the amplitude equations hold, and unlike \(E(t)\) it can be made stationary in every parameter: in \(\lambda\) by the amplitude equations, in \(t\) by choosing \(\lambda\).

$$\bra{0}(1+\Lambda)\,(\Hb-E)\,\ket{\nu}=0\ \ \text{for all }\nu\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \bm J^{\tr}\boldsymbol\lambda=-\pd{E}{\bm t},\quad J_{\mu\nu}=\pd{\Omega_\mu}{t_\nu}.\tag{2}$$

THE \(\Lambda\) EQUATIONS [10, 11]. The condition \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial t=0\). Linear in \(\lambda\), with the transposed amplitude Jacobian; \(\bra{0}(1+\Lambda)\) is the left eigenvector of the non-Hermitian \(\Hb\). Contains no perturbation, so it is solved once, at about the cost of one CCSD.

$$\frac{\dd E}{\dd x}=\bra{0}(1+\Lambda)\,\Hb^{x}\,\ket{0}=\sum_{pq}\gamma_{pq}\,h^{x}_{pq}+\tfrac14\sum_{pqrs}\Gamma_{pqrs}\,\langle pq\|rs\rangle^{x},\qquad \gamma_{pq}=\bra{0}(1+\Lambda)\,e^{-T}a_p^\dagger a_q\,e^{T}\ket{0}.\tag{3}$$

THE GRADIENT [12, 15, 16]. A Hellmann–Feynman-like expectation value with a different bra and ket. No \(\dd t/\dd x\) appears: that term multiplies \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial t\), which (2) made zero. The densities \(\gamma\) and \(\Gamma\) are built once from \(t\) and \(\lambda\); the perturbation enters only through the derivative integrals.

$$\bm A^{\tr}\bm z=\bm X,\qquad X_{ai}\propto F^{\rm gen}_{ai}-F^{\rm gen}_{ia},\qquad \frac{\dd E}{\dd x}=\sum_{\mu\nu}D_{\mu\nu}h^{x}_{\mu\nu}+\tfrac12\sum_{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}\Gamma^{\rm eff}_{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}(\mu\nu|\lambda\sigma)^{x}-\sum_{\mu\nu}W_{\mu\nu}S^{x}_{\mu\nu}+\frac{\dd V_{nn}}{\dd x}.\tag{4}$$

THE Z-VECTOR AND THE WORKING FORMULA [22–24]. The orbitals move with the nuclei, and \(E\) is not stationary to orbital rotations, so the orbital response \(\bm U^{x}\) would be needed for every \(x\). One solve with the transposed orbital Hessian \(\bm A\) against the CC orbital gradient \(\bm X\) gives \(\bm z\), which folds into the relaxed density \(\bm D\), the effective two-particle density and the energy-weighted density \(\bm W\), and the gradient takes the shape of a Hartree–Fock gradient.

The recipe, in the order a code runs it

Hartree–Fock → CCSD amplitudes \(t\) → \(\Lambda\) equations (2) for \(\lambda\) → unrelaxed densities \(\gamma\), \(\Gamma\) → generalized Fock matrix and orbital gradient \(\bm X\) → Z-vector equation (4) for \(\bm z\) → relaxed \(\bm D\), \(\bm\Gamma^{\rm eff}\), \(\bm W\) back-transformed to the atomic-orbital basis → contract with derivative integrals for every coordinate. Two iterative solves of CCSD size and one of CPHF size, whatever the number of atoms; central differences would need \(6N_{\rm at}\) energies.

The four questions, with answers

  1. 1Why do you need \(\Lambda\) at all?

    Because the CC energy is not variational in the amplitudes: \(\partial E/\partial t\neq0\), so the chain rule leaves \(\sum_\mu(\partial E/\partial t_\mu)(\dd t_\mu/\dd x)\) in the gradient. The multipliers \(\lambda\) are chosen so that the Lagrangian, which equals the energy, has no first-order dependence on \(t\). Equivalently, \(\Hb\) is not Hermitian, so its left eigenvector \(\bra{0}(1+\Lambda)\) must be found separately from the ket \(e^{T}\ket{0}\).

  2. 2Why is there no need to solve for the amplitude derivatives?

    Because \(\mathcal L\) is stationary in \(t\). A first-order change in \(t\) changes \(\mathcal L\) only at second order, so \(\dd t/\dd x\) drops out of a first derivative. This is the \(2n+1\) rule: wavefunction parameters to order \(n\) give energies to order \(2n+1\). Solving for \(\dd t/\dd x\) instead would mean one Jacobian solve per coordinate, \(3N_{\rm at}\) of them, each the cost of a CCSD.

  3. 3What does the Z-vector equation solve for, and what does it replace?

    It solves \(\bm A^{\tr}\bm z=\bm X\) for \(\bm z\), the Lagrange multipliers of the Brillouin conditions, with \(\bm A\) the coupled-perturbed Hartree–Fock orbital Hessian and \(\bm X\) the CC orbital gradient. It replaces solving \(\bm A\bm U^{x}=\bm B^{x}\) once per coordinate: \(\bm X^{\tr}\bm A^{-1}\bm B^{x}\) becomes \(\bm z^{\tr}\bm B^{x}\), one solve and cheap contractions, and \(\bm z\) ends up inside the relaxed and energy-weighted densities of (4).

  4. 4Why are the \(\Lambda\) equations solved once rather than once per nuclear coordinate?

    Because they do not contain the perturbation. Equation (2) is \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial t=0\) at the reference geometry and depends only on \(\Hb\) and \(t\). The same holds for \(\bm z\). The coordinate \(x\) enters only through the derivative integrals in the final contraction of (3) and (4), which is why an analytic gradient costs a constant number of energies, independent of the number of atoms.

Three traps

  • \(\Lambda\) is not \(T^\dagger\). They agree to first order in perturbation theory and differ beyond it; only the true \(\Lambda\) makes \(\mathcal L\) stationary, and an approximate one gives forces that are not the derivative of any energy.
  • Converge \(t\) and \(\lambda\) more tightly for a gradient than for an energy. The energy error is second order in the residuals, the gradient error is first order.
  • An unrelaxed property stops after the densities of (3); a relaxed one adds the Z-vector. For nuclear gradients there is no choice, the basis moves with the nuclei and relaxation is mandatory.
