Response versus Relaxation

Orbital response and orbital relaxation describe the same physical effect from two angles, but in coupled cluster derivative theory the words have settled into distinct roles. This note fixes them, and separates the two different relaxations that a CC gradient contains.

Notation

\(x\)
a perturbation strength: a nuclear coordinate or an external field component.
\(\bm U^{x}\)
the first-order orbital response, \(\bm C(x)=\bm C(0)(\bm 1+\bm U^{x}+\cdots)\), from coupled-perturbed Hartree–Fock.
\(\bm X,\ \bm z,\ \bm A,\ \bm B^{x}\)
the CC orbital gradient, the Z-vector, the HF orbital Hessian and the CPHF right-hand side, as in the gradient notes.
\(T_1\)
the singles cluster operator; \(e^{T_1}\ket{0}\) is a rotated determinant by Thouless’ theorem.
\(\gamma,\ \bm D\)
the unrelaxed CC response density and the relaxed density that adds the Z-vector contribution.

The whole answer in three lines

  1. 1
    Response is a derivative: the matrix \(\bm U^{x}\) that says how the orbitals change per unit perturbation. Relaxation is the adjustment that derivative describes, and the contribution it makes to an energy derivative or a property.
  2. 2
    A CC gradient contains two different relaxations: the singles relax the orbitals with respect to correlation, inside the wavefunction; the Z-vector carries the response of the reference orbitals to the perturbation. The first never removes the need for the second.
  3. 3
    “Relaxed” and “unrelaxed” densities refer only to the second kind. An unrelaxed CC density already contains the singles relaxation; what it lacks is the orbital response to the perturbation.

1A derivative and what it describes

When a nucleus moves or a field is switched on, the Hartree–Fock orbitals are re-optimized in the new Hamiltonian. The orbital response is the first-order part of that change,

$$\bm C(x)=\bm C(0)\bigl[\bm 1+\bm U^{x}+\mathcal O(x^2)\bigr],\qquad\bm A\,\bm U^{x}=\bm B^{x},\tag{1}$$

a definite matrix obtained from a definite linear equation. Orbital relaxation is the physical adjustment that \(\bm U^{x}\) quantifies, and, in a derivative expression, the term it produces. In a CC gradient that term is

$$\Bigl(\frac{\dd E}{\dd x}\Bigr)_{\rm relaxation}=\sum_{ai}X_{ai}\,U^{x}_{ai}=\bm z^{\tr}\bm B^{x},\qquad\bm A^{\tr}\bm z=\bm X,\tag{2}$$

and the Z-vector identity on the right is the whole reason the two words come apart in practice. The response \(\bm U^{x}\) is never computed; the relaxation contribution is, through \(\bm z\), without any reference to a particular perturbation.

The same distinction separates finite and infinitesimal perturbations. In a finite-field or displaced-geometry calculation the orbitals relax fully and nobody speaks of a response. In analytic derivative theory the first-order response is all that enters a first derivative, by the \(2n+1\) rule applied to the orbital parameters, and “relaxation contribution” means exactly the term (2).

2Two relaxations in one gradient

This is the distinction that matters most, and the one most easily blurred. A coupled cluster gradient contains orbital relaxation of two different kinds, with different origins and different machinery.

Key distinction

Relaxation inside the wavefunction, through \(T_1\). By Thouless’ theorem the singles rotate the reference orbitals in the presence of the pair correlations described by \(T_2\). This is relaxation with respect to correlation itself, not with respect to any external perturbation. It is contained in the converged amplitudes and needs no response equation.

Relaxation of the reference orbitals under the perturbation, through the Z-vector. The Hartree–Fock orbitals change when \(x\) changes, and because the CC energy is not stationary with respect to orbital rotations, that change contributes to the derivative. The Z-vector computes the contribution (2) once, for all perturbations.

The first does not remove the need for the second. Converged singles make the CC wavefunction as good as it would be with a rotated reference, but they do not make the energy stationary in the orbital parameters: the orbital gradient \(\bm X\) is nonzero at convergence, so the response \(\bm U^{x}\) still multiplies something. Only orbital-optimized CC, which imposes \(\bm X=0\) as a condition on the orbitals, eliminates the Z-vector step. Brueckner CC, which imposes \(T_1=0\) instead, does not: as the gradient notes explain, vanishing singles is not a stationarity condition, and an orbital response equation built on the Brueckner condition remains.

A useful way to keep the two apart is to ask what each one responds to. The singles respond to correlation and are determined by the amplitude equations. The Z-vector responds to the energy’s lack of stationarity and is determined by the orbital Hessian. One is part of the wavefunction; the other is part of the derivative.

3Relaxed and unrelaxed densities

The CC response density of the gradient notes,

$$\gamma_{pq}=\bra{0}(1+\Lambda)e^{-T}a_p^\dagger a_q e^{T}\ket{0},\tag{3}$$

is called the unrelaxed density. The relaxed density adds the Z-vector to its virtual–occupied block,

$$\bm D=\mathrm{sym}\,\gamma+\bm z\quad\text{(virtual–occupied block)},\tag{4}$$

and is the one that appears in the gradient. The names refer only to the second kind of relaxation of Section 2. The table says what each density contains:

DensityContains the singles relaxation, through the singles amplitudes and multipliersContains the orbital response to the perturbation, through the Z-vectorIs the exact derivative of

So an unrelaxed density is not a density without orbital relaxation. It is a density without the orbital response to the perturbation, which is a narrower statement, and the naming is unfortunate but entrenched.

4An illustration with a dipole moment

Add a uniform field to the Hamiltonian, \(H(F)=H-F\hat\mu\), and consider the derivative of the CC energy with respect to \(F\) at \(F=0\), which is the dipole moment up to sign. There are two finite-field calculations one could run, and they give the two densities of Section 3.

  • Orbitals frozen. Solve Hartree–Fock without the field, then run CC with \(H(F)\) for several small \(F\) and differentiate numerically. The orbitals never see the field. The result is the unrelaxed dipole, $$\mu_{\rm unrel}=\sum_{pq}\gamma_{pq}\,\mu_{pq},\tag{5}$$ which includes the singles relaxation, since \(T_1\) and \(\Lambda_1\) adjust to the field through the amplitude and \(\Lambda\) equations, but not the orbital response.
  • Orbitals re-optimized. Solve Hartree–Fock in the field at each \(F\), then run CC on those orbitals and differentiate. The result is the relaxed dipole, $$\mu_{\rm rel}=\sum_{pq}D_{pq}\,\mu_{pq},\tag{6}$$ which is what the analytic Z-vector machinery produces in one calculation.

The difference between (5) and (6) is the term \(\bm z^{\tr}\bm B^{F}\) of (2) with the field as the perturbation. It is usually small for electronic dipoles of closed-shell molecules near equilibrium, a few percent, because the singles have already done most of the relaxing; Section 7 describes a case where it is not small. For nuclear gradients the comparison cannot even be set up, because the basis functions move with the nuclei and the orbitals cannot be held fixed in any meaningful sense. A nuclear gradient is always relaxed.

5When relaxation is left out on purpose

For frequency-dependent properties the two densities lead to two different response theories, and the unrelaxed one is the standard choice. Including the Hartree–Fock orbital response in a CC linear response function introduces poles at the time-dependent Hartree–Fock excitation energies, which are spurious: the physical poles of a CC response function should sit at the CC excitation energies, which come from the Jacobian alone. Koch and Jørgensen showed that the orbital-unrelaxed formulation, in which the orbitals are fixed and only the amplitudes respond, has the correct pole structure, and this is how CC response theory and equation-of-motion CC are built. Static properties and gradients, by contrast, use the relaxed formulation, because there the orbital response is a genuine part of the derivative and introduces no poles.

The lesson for reading papers is that “unrelaxed” is not a synonym for “approximate”. For a static property it is the derivative of a slightly different energy functional; for a dynamic property it is the formulation with the right physics.

6Other meanings in the literature

The word relaxation carries at least three meanings in electronic structure, and only two of them have appeared so far.

  • Relaxation with respect to correlation, through singles or Brueckner orbitals, as in Section 2. No perturbation is involved.
  • Relaxation in response to a perturbation, through \(\bm U^{x}\) or \(\bm z\), as in Sections 1, 3 and 4. This is the meaning in “relaxed density”.
  • Relaxation upon ionization or excitation, as in the \(\Delta\)SCF literature: the energy lowering when the orbitals of an ion or an excited state are re-optimized rather than taken from the neutral ground state. Koopmans’ theorem errors are discussed in terms of this relaxation and of correlation, and no derivative is involved at all.

“Response” is more uniform. It almost always means a derivative of something with respect to a perturbation: orbital response for \(\bm U^{x}\), amplitude response for \(\dd t/\dd x\), and response functions for the frequency-dependent derivatives of the energy in response theory. When a paper says “orbital response” it means a solved linear equation. When it says “orbital relaxation” you have to decide from context which of the three meanings is in play.

7In multicomponent calculations

Both kinds of relaxation appear for each species in NEO-CCSD. The protonic singles relax the proton orbital with respect to electron–proton correlation, which the Thouless notes show is a large effect because the NEO-HF proton orbital is a poor starting point. The protonic Z-vector carries the proton orbital’s response to a nuclear displacement or a field, which the NEO gradient notes show is also large, for the same physical reason. Neither substitutes for the other, and the difference between unrelaxed and relaxed protonic properties is correspondingly larger than for electronic ones. The dipole example of Section 4 applied to a quantum proton is a case where (5) and (6) differ by far more than a few percent.
