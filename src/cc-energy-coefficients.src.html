CC Energy Coefficients

Where the 1, the ¼ and the ½ in the coupled cluster energy expression come from, what happens to them under other conventions, and how to check them by hand.

Notation

\(i,j,k\;/\;a,b,c\;/\;p,q,r,s\)
occupied / virtual / general spin orbitals; \(\ket{0}\) the reference, \(E_0=\bra{0}H\ket{0}\).
\(\langle pq\|rs\rangle\)
antisymmetrized integral \(\langle pq|rs\rangle-\langle pq|sr\rangle\); antisymmetric under \(p\leftrightarrow q\) and under \(r\leftrightarrow s\).
\(\tau_i^a,\;\tau_{ij}^{ab}\)
\(a_a^\dagger a_i\) and \(a_a^\dagger a_b^\dagger a_ja_i\); \(\tau_{ij}^{ab}\ket{0}=\ket{\Phi_{ij}^{ab}}\).
\(t_i^a,\;t_{ij}^{ab}\)
amplitudes; \(t_{ij}^{ab}=-t_{ji}^{ab}=-t_{ij}^{ba}\).
\(\sum_{ijab}\) versus \(\sum_{i\lt j,\,a\lt b}\)
unrestricted sum over all index values versus restricted sum over distinct excitations.

The formula under discussion

$$E=E_0+\sum_{ia}f_{ia}\,t_i^a+\tfrac14\sum_{ijab}\langle ij\|ab\rangle\,t_{ij}^{ab}+\tfrac12\sum_{ijab}\langle ij\|ab\rangle\,t_i^a t_j^b\tag{1}$$
  1. 1
    The structure \(E=\bra{0}H(1+T_1+T_2+\tfrac12T_1^2)\ket{0}\) comes from the exponential; the ½ is the \(1/2!\) of its series.
  2. ¼
    The ¼ is the normalization of \(T_2\) written as an unrestricted sum over four equivalent index orderings. It is the same ¼ that stands in front of the two-electron part of \(H\).
  3. 1
    Every term is a Slater-rule matrix element, \(f_{ia}\) or \(\langle ij\|ab\rangle\), times the coefficient of the corresponding determinant in the wavefunction. The energy is \(\bra{0}H\ket{\Psi}\) and nothing more.

1The three terms

The energy is the projection of the Schrödinger equation onto the reference,

$$E=\bra{0}He^{T}\ket{0}=\bra{0}H\Bigl(1+T+\tfrac12T^2+\tfrac16T^3+\cdots\Bigr)\ket{0}.\tag{2}$$

A two-body Hamiltonian connects the reference only to the reference, singles and doubles:

$$\bra{0}H\ket{0}=E_0,\qquad\bra{0}H\ket{\Phi_i^a}=f_{ia},\qquad\bra{0}H\ket{\Phi_{ij}^{ab}}=\langle ij\|ab\rangle,\qquad\bra{0}H\ket{\Phi_{ijk}^{abc}}=0.\tag{3}$$

With \(T=T_1+T_2+\cdots\), the only pieces of (2) that produce excitations of rank two or less are \(T_1\), \(T_2\), and \(\tfrac12T_1^2\). Everything else, \(T_3\), \(T_1T_2\), \(\tfrac12T_2^2\), \(\tfrac16T_1^3\) and so on, lands on triples or higher and is annihilated by \(\bra{0}H\). Hence

Key result · the structure before any integrals

$$E=E_0+\bra{0}HT_1\ket{0}+\bra{0}HT_2\ket{0}+\tfrac12\bra{0}HT_1^2\ket{0}.\tag{4}$$

Three terms, with coefficients 1, 1 and ½. The ½ is the \(1/2!\) of the exponential series and has no other origin. This structure is the same for CCSD, CCSDT and every higher level, since higher clusters cannot reach the reference through \(H\) directly; they influence the energy only by changing \(t_i^a\) and \(t_{ij}^{ab}\).

2Unrestricted sums and antisymmetry

The second source of numbers is the convention for writing operators with antisymmetric indices. The two-electron part of the Hamiltonian is

$$H_2=\tfrac14\sum_{pqrs}\langle pq\|rs\rangle\,a_p^\dagger a_q^\dagger a_sa_r .\tag{5}$$

Swapping \(p\leftrightarrow q\) changes the sign of the integral and the sign of the operator string, so the term is unchanged; the same holds for \(r\leftrightarrow s\). The unrestricted sum therefore visits each physically distinct term four times, and the ¼ corrects the count. Written with non-antisymmetrized integrals the same operator reads \(\tfrac12\sum\langle pq|rs\rangle a_p^\dagger a_q^\dagger a_sa_r\), where only the simultaneous swap of both pairs is a symmetry.

The doubles cluster operator follows the identical convention,

$$T_2=\tfrac14\sum_{ijab}t_{ij}^{ab}\,\tau_{ij}^{ab}=\sum_{i\lt j,\;a\lt b}t_{ij}^{ab}\,\tau_{ij}^{ab},\tag{6}$$

because \(t_{ij}^{ab}\) and \(\tau_{ij}^{ab}\) are both antisymmetric in each index pair, so the orderings \((ij,ab)\), \((ji,ab)\), \((ij,ba)\), \((ji,ba)\) are the same operator. The general rule: a rank-\(n\) object with \(n\) antisymmetric hole indices and \(n\) antisymmetric particle indices carries \(1/(n!)^2\) in front of an unrestricted sum. That is 1 for singles, ¼ for doubles, and \(1/36\) for triples, which is the prefactor of the (T) energy formula.

Where the ¼ of the Hamiltonian goes

The matrix element \(\bra{0}H\ket{\Phi_{ij}^{ab}}=\langle ij\|ab\rangle\) in (3) has coefficient 1, not ¼, even though \(H_2\) carries ¼. Evaluating it from (5), the only surviving terms are those with \(\{p,q\}=\{i,j\}\) and \(\{r,s\}=\{a,b\}\): four index orderings, each contributing \(\langle ij\|ab\rangle\) once the signs of the integral and of the operator string are taken into account. The ¼ of \(H\) is consumed by those four orderings inside the Slater rule. This is why the energy (1) contains exactly one ¼ in its doubles term, inherited from \(T_2\), and not \(1/16\).

3Each term in turn

The singles term: coefficient 1

$$\bra{0}HT_1\ket{0}=\sum_{ia}t_i^a\,\bra{0}H\ket{\Phi_i^a}=\sum_{ia}f_{ia}\,t_i^a .\tag{7}$$

A single excitation has one hole and one particle index and no symmetry to compensate, so each appears once and the coefficient is 1. The Slater rule gives \(\bra{0}H\ket{\Phi_i^a}=h_{ia}+\sum_j\langle ij\|aj\rangle=f_{ia}\). For a Hartree–Fock reference this vanishes by Brillouin’s theorem; for any other reference, such as DFT or ROHF orbitals, it is the first-order coupling of the singles to the energy.

The doubles term: coefficient ¼

$$\bra{0}HT_2\ket{0}=\tfrac14\sum_{ijab}t_{ij}^{ab}\,\bra{0}H\ket{\Phi_{ij}^{ab}}=\tfrac14\sum_{ijab}\langle ij\|ab\rangle\,t_{ij}^{ab}=\sum_{i\lt j,\;a\lt b}\langle ij\|ab\rangle\,t_{ij}^{ab}.\tag{8}$$

The ¼ is the one from (6), carried through unchanged. In the restricted form it is simply the integral times the amplitude, once per distinct double excitation.

The quadratic singles term: coefficient ½

Here the ½ is the \(1/2!\) of the exponential. The work is in evaluating the operator. Write out the square as an unrestricted double sum over single excitations:

$$\tfrac12\bra{0}HT_1^2\ket{0}=\tfrac12\sum_{ia}\sum_{jb}t_i^at_j^b\,\bra{0}H\,\tau_i^a\tau_j^b\ket{0}.\tag{9}$$

Two single excitations compose into a double. Anticommuting \(a_i\) past \(a_b^\dagger\) and then \(a_i\) past \(a_j\),

$$\tau_i^a\tau_j^b=a_a^\dagger a_i\,a_b^\dagger a_j=-a_a^\dagger a_b^\dagger a_ia_j=a_a^\dagger a_b^\dagger a_ja_i=\tau_{ij}^{ab},\tag{10}$$

for \(i\neq j\) and \(a\neq b\), and zero otherwise. So \(\bra{0}H\tau_i^a\tau_j^b\ket{0}=\langle ij\|ab\rangle\) by (3), which gives the last term of (1):

$$\tfrac12\bra{0}HT_1^2\ket{0}=\tfrac12\sum_{ijab}\langle ij\|ab\rangle\,t_i^at_j^b .\tag{11}$$

It is worth seeing how this unrestricted sum counts a given distinct determinant \(\ket{\Phi_{ij}^{ab}}\) with \(i\lt j\) and \(a\lt b\). Four terms of (11) reach it:

\((i,a)\,(j,b)\)\(t_i^at_j^b\langle ij\|ab\rangle\)\(+\,t_i^at_j^b\,\langle ij\|ab\rangle\)

\((i,b)\,(j,a)\)\(t_i^bt_j^a\langle ij\|ba\rangle\)\(-\,t_i^bt_j^a\,\langle ij\|ab\rangle\)
\((j,a)\,(i,b)\)\(t_j^at_i^b\langle ji\|ab\rangle\)\(-\,t_i^bt_j^a\,\langle ij\|ab\rangle\)

Their sum is \(2\langle ij\|ab\rangle\bigl(t_i^at_j^b-t_i^bt_j^a\bigr)\), and the ½ in front reduces it to one copy per distinct determinant:

$$\tfrac12\sum_{ijab}\langle ij\|ab\rangle\,t_i^at_j^b=\sum_{i\lt j,\;a\lt b}\langle ij\|ab\rangle\bigl(t_i^at_j^b-t_i^bt_j^a\bigr)=\tfrac14\sum_{ijab}\langle ij\|ab\rangle\bigl(t_i^at_j^b-t_i^bt_j^a\bigr).\tag{12}$$

The three forms are identical. The first is how (1) is usually printed; the third puts the quadratic term on the same footing as the \(T_2\) term, with the antisymmetrized product of singles in the role of a doubles amplitude.

4The energy as a projection of the wavefunction

Combining (8) and (12), the energy collapses to one statement:

Key result · the energy is the doubles coefficient contracted with the integrals

$$E=E_0+\sum_{ia}f_{ia}\,c_i^a+\sum_{i\lt j,\;a\lt b}\langle ij\|ab\rangle\,c_{ij}^{ab},\qquad c_i^a=t_i^a,\qquad c_{ij}^{ab}=t_{ij}^{ab}+t_i^at_j^b-t_i^bt_j^a .\tag{13}$$

Here \(c_i^a\) and \(c_{ij}^{ab}\) are the coefficients of the singly and doubly excited determinants in \(e^{T}\ket{0}\). Equation (13) is just \(E=\bra{0}H\ket{\Psi}\) with the Slater rules applied, which is what the projected Schrödinger equation says it should be. The doubles coefficient is the CI-to-CC relation \(C_2=T_2+\tfrac12T_1^2\) written out in indices, and the combination \(t_{ij}^{ab}+t_i^at_j^b-t_i^bt_j^a\) is exactly the \(\tau_{ij}^{ab}\) intermediate of the Stanton–Gauss working equations. A code that forms \(\tau\) once and contracts it with \(\langle ij\|ab\rangle\) has computed the whole correlation energy.

The product form of the exponential from the notes on exponentials, \(e^{T}=\prod_\mu(1+t_\mu\tau_\mu)\), gives the same coefficient by inspection: the determinant \(\Phi_{ij}^{ab}\) is reached once by the double excitation \(\mu=(ij\to ab)\), with amplitude \(t_{ij}^{ab}\), and once by each of the two pairs of singles that compose into it, \((i\to a)(j\to b)\) with product \(t_i^at_j^b\) and \((i\to b)(j\to a)\) with product \(t_i^bt_j^a\) and a minus sign from (10).

5The same numbers from diagrams

Diagrammatic derivations never write unrestricted sums or exponential series; they draw each distinct contraction once and attach weight factors by rule. The two relevant rules are: a factor ½ for every pair of equivalent lines, two lines of the same type that begin at the same vertex and end at the same vertex; and a factor \(1/k!\) for \(k\) equivalent vertices, identical cluster vertices connected in the same way. Applied to the energy:

Energy diagramEquivalent linesEquivalent verticesWeight
\(H\) joined to one \(T_1\) vertexnonenone1
\(H\) joined to one \(T_2\) vertextwo hole lines, two particle lines: two pairsnone½ · ½ = ¼
\(H\) joined to two \(T_1\) verticesnone, the lines go to different verticesthe two \(T_1\) vertices1/2! = ½

The algebra of Sections 2 and 3 and the diagram rules are the same counting seen from two sides. The equivalent-line factor is the \(1/(n!)^2\) of the unrestricted sum, and the equivalent-vertex factor is the \(1/k!\) of the exponential series. When the two bookkeepings disagree in a derivation, one of them has counted an ordering twice.

6Other conventions

The coefficients in (1) belong to one convention: spin orbitals, antisymmetrized integrals, and unrestricted sums. Change the convention and the numbers change with it.

  • Closed-shell spatial orbitals. Summing over spin and using spatial amplitudes \(t_{ij}^{ab}\) that are no longer antisymmetric gives $$E_{\rm corr}=\sum_{ijab}\bigl[2(ia|jb)-(ib|ja)\bigr]\bigl(t_{ij}^{ab}+t_i^at_j^b\bigr),\tag{14}$$ with chemists’ integrals, no ¼ and no ½, and the exchange product \(t_i^bt_j^a\) gone from the parenthesis. The 2 and the −1 are what remains of spin integration. This is the form most closed-shell codes implement.
  • Restricted sums. Writing every sum over \(i\lt j\), \(a\lt b\) removes the ¼ and turns the ½ into the antisymmetrized product, as in (13). Some authors print the energy this way, and the ¼ and ½ then never appear.
  • Mixed electron–proton terms. In multicomponent CCSD the electron–proton contribution to the energy is \(-\sum_{iIaA}(ia|IA)\bigl(t_{iI}^{aA}+t_i^at_I^A\bigr)\), with coefficient 1. An electronic index and a protonic index cannot be interchanged, so there is only one ordering per term and no symmetry factor to compensate.
  • Perturbative triples. The \(1/36\) in the (T) energy formula is \(1/(3!)^2\), the rank-three case of the rule in Section 2.

If a coefficient in a paper or a code looks wrong, the first thing to check is which of these conventions is in force. Most apparent discrepancies are a ¼ that has been absorbed into a restricted sum or a 2 that has come from spin integration.

7A check by hand

Take the two-electron, two-orbital system of the refresher: occupied spin orbitals \(g\alpha,g\beta\), virtual spin orbitals \(u\alpha,u\beta\), and a single doubles amplitude \(t=t_{g\alpha g\beta}^{u\alpha u\beta}\). The exact correlation energy is \(Kt\), with \(K=\langle g\alpha\,g\beta\|u\alpha\,u\beta\rangle\). The unrestricted sum in (8) runs over sixteen index combinations, of which four are nonzero:

\((i,j,a,b)\)\(\langle ij\|ab\rangle\)\(t_{ij}^{ab}\)Product
\((g\alpha,g\beta,u\alpha,u\beta)\)\(+K\)\(+t\)\(+Kt\)
\((g\beta,g\alpha,u\alpha,u\beta)\)\(-K\)\(-t\)\(+Kt\)
\((g\alpha,g\beta,u\beta,u\alpha)\)\(-K\)\(-t\)\(+Kt\)
\((g\beta,g\alpha,u\beta,u\alpha)\)\(+K\)\(+t\)\(+Kt\)

The sum is \(4Kt\), and the ¼ brings it back to \(Kt\). The same exercise with the \(T_1^2\) term, for a system where singles are allowed, reproduces the table in Section 3. Doing this once, with real indices, is the fastest way to make the coefficients stop looking arbitrary.
